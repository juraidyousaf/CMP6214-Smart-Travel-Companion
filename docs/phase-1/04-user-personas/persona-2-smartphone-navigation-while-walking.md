| Behaviour 1 | On one unfamiliar residential path in Canterbury, 20 adults remembered landmarks and the path better with AR glasses than with a phone. Both used the researchers’ landmark-based prototype. Memory-test groups were unequal. Lakehal et al. (2023), S004. Awaiting your verification |
| Authors’ interpretation | The S004 authors suggest the phone pulled the gaze down. That is their interpretation of this prototype on one path. It is not a finding about all tourists or all navigation apps |
| Behaviour 2 | Across 22 studies and 592 people, phone use while walking was associated with slower gait and shorter steps. Reading and texting changed speed more than talking or calling. Older adults did not show the same step-length and step-time effects. The task and the setting matter. This is pedestrian safety research, not tourism. Zhang et al. (2023), S017. Awaiting your verification |
| Behaviour 3 | Easier mobile navigation is linked with less attention to the surroundings and weaker spatial learning. “Easier and faster” is not every pedestrian’s goal. A small phone screen makes distant landmarks harder to keep in view. Google Maps is named only as an app that lets people change scale, not as a product test. Ruginski et al. (2022), S006. Awaiting your verification |
| Goal | **Your interpretation, not a quotation or an established finding:** follow guidance on foot while still being able to notice the place, rather than only taking the next turn.
