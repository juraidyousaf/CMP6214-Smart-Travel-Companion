## What you are asking to rely on

| Approach | What you actually do | What it can support | What it cannot support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Published user feedback | Read public reviews of travel or map apps. Copy quotations exactly. Record URL, date, and product. | Documented frustrations and requests, in the reviewer’s words | A persona with a name, age, and life story. Reviews are not interviews |
| Public travel usability research | Search BCU Library and Google Scholar. Read papers on tourist apps or mobile usability while travelling | Patterns the authors report, if you cite them accurately | Your own users’ behaviour |
| Pedestrian navigation studies | Search for research on walking navigation, wayfinding in unfamiliar cities, and phone use while walking | Evidence about on-foot navigation problems | A claim that your feature list is validated |
| Accessible interaction research | Search for pedestrian navigation or mobile travel research that includes disabled people, and for accessibility guidance | A carefully sourced accessibility persona, if the lecturer accepts secondary evidence | Lived experience you did not gather. Do not generalise one paper to “all” disabled tourists |
| Direct observation of existing apps | Use the products. Attempt set tasks. Write what happened. Screenshot as you go | Competitor analysis and some journey pain, observed by you | Other people’s thoughts. Write only what you saw and did |
| Documentary competitor analysis | Compare observation notes across products using the framework in `docs/phase-1/03-competitor-analysis/` | A sourced competitor section | Market share or user-base claims unless a published source states them |

## Suggested sequence before 23 October 2026

1. Send the lecturer email on 18 October 2026.
2. Run three observation sessions of real products. Candidate products are listed in the competitor framework. You choose the three. One session is one product, about 20–30 minutes, with a task written down first.
3. Sample public reviews for those same products. A small set you actually read is better than a large set of titles. Record the star rating context if it is visible, and do not pretend a handful of reviews is a survey.
4. Read a short list of academic or official sources aimed at H1–H4 in `PRD.md`. Start with pedestrian navigation and with one accessibility topic. The accessibility topic should follow what the literature you find actually discusses. Do not pick an impairment first and then hunt only for agreement.
5. Stop and fill the evidence review in `research/research-notes.md` before drafting personas.

## How notes become checklist text

| Checklist section | Permitted input if the lecturer agrees | Still your job |
|---|---|---|
| Market research | Published figures and research you read | A short synthesis, with limits stated |
| Competitor analysis | Your observation log and screenshots | Comparison in your words |
| Personas | Patterns across sources, each pattern cited | Three distinct, evidence-linked personas. No fictional biographies presented as fact |
| Accessibility persona | Studies and documented accounts you read, plus accessibility standards you checked | Needs and mobile implications traced to those sources |
| Jobs-to-be-done | Situations evidenced in sources or in your observation | At least one job per persona, written by you |
| Journey maps | Tasks you observed or that sources describe, clearly marked | Three maps, including premium purchase and smart glasses as the brief requires |
| MoSCoW | Requirements implied by the evidence and the brief’s mandatory scenarios | Priority judgements, with “Won’t” items included |
| HTA | Tasks from the journeys | Diagrams you draw |

Where a journey step exists only because the brief requires it (checkout, for example) and your sources do not mention it, label that step as a coursework requirement rather than a user finding.

## Quality bar

- Every quotation has a source and is accurate.
- Every paraphrase can be checked against a page, section, or screenshot.
- Sample sizes in published studies stay attached to the claim. Do not drop them.
- One observation session is one session. Do not write “users struggle” from your own single attempt. Write “in this session I…” .
- If sources conflict, keep the conflict in the write-up.

## If the lecturer says no

Follow the method they set. If they require primary research you cannot complete in time, ask what partial submission they want, and record the answer. Do not back-fill with fictional primary data.

## If the lecturer does not reply in time
