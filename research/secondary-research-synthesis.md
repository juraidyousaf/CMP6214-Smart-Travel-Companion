## 6. Verified evidence

### RQ2 and RQ5 — phones, gaze, and AR glasses

**S004.** Lakehal et al. (2023) compared landmark-based pedestrian navigation on a smartphone and on AR glasses with 20 participants aged 21–39. The walk was an unfamiliar residential path in Canterbury, not a tourist itinerary. Memory tests were taken after the assisted walk and again a week later, including a second walk without the device. The AR-glasses group remembered landmarks and paths better than the smartphone group. The authors suggest one reason: glasses can leave the walker’s view on the street, while a phone pulls the gaze down. They also report that both groups gave higher System Usability Scale scores after use, and that no blocking usability problem was found in those scores. They limit generalisation: one path, a small sample, and unequal group sizes on the memory test.

Their prototype placed guidance at the bottom centre of the glasses and used green. That is a study-design choice, not a requirement for this project.

**S014, abstract only.** Ishikawa et al. (2008) report that participants who walked six unfamiliar routes with a GPS phone travelled longer distances and stopped more often than people using a paper map or people who had already walked the route with a guide. Compared with the direct-experience group, GPS users were slower, made larger direction errors, drew less accurate sketch maps, and rated the task as more difficult. The full article was not opened. Do not add sample size or discussion points from memory. Read the PDF through BCU Library before you cite details beyond this abstract.

**S006.** Ruginski et al. (2022) review navigation research and state that there is no single accessible interface. They also note research in which mobile navigation is linked with poorer spatial learning because attention is pulled away from the environment, while warning that “easier and faster” is not every pedestrian’s goal. Someone who wants to understand a place may need a different design from someone who only wants the next turn.

### RQ5 — AR wayfinding is promising and uneven

**S005.** Qiu, Mostafavi and Kalantari (2025) review real-world AR wayfinding studies. The abstract says 88 studies were analysed. The methods text on the same page later refers to 65 articles being divided for review. Check which figure belongs to which stage before you quote a number.

From the opened paper:

- Outdoor studies that reported a localisation method used GPS.
- Head-mounted displays are described as hands-free. Handheld AR still has to be held.
- Across the studies they review, visual AR guidance tended to be more effective than auditory cues, and overview maps improved AR performance.
- Wayfinding-performance gains for AR were more consistent indoors than outdoors.
- Users often rated AR navigation positively and also reported hardware, localisation, and interface problems.
- The review discusses cognitive load as mixed: some wearable AR studies report lower load, and a poorly designed or unfamiliar interface can add load.

This review is not a test of a tourism product and does not show that a premium glasses subscription would be wanted.

### RQ1 and RQ3 — landmarks, clutter, and split tools

**S006** recommends that mobile navigation:

- offer both local turn information and a wider orientation, including landmarks and cardinal directions
- let the person choose track-up or north-up
- avoid clutter
- chunk spoken directions, including landmark-based chunking
- point to landmarks at decisions, not only to distances
- repeat information in more than one sense, including sound or vibration where sight is limited
- include physical access information such as wheelchair-friendly routes
- include non-visual cues such as sound, touch, and smell
- avoid giving more information at once than a person can use

The same paper says limited phone screens make it hard to keep distant landmarks in view, even when the person can pan and zoom. It names Google Maps as an example of an application that lets people change scale. That is a description of a common feature, not a usability test.

**S007** reports, from earlier focus groups it cites, that blind and deafblind travellers sometimes need more than one app for a single trip because apps are not integrated. That claim is about those focus groups, not about every tourist.

### RQ1 — choosing what to visit

**S015.** Liu, Mehraliyev, Liu and Schuckert (2020) interviewed 21 people who had recently travelled. They report that attractions were roughly planned before the trip, using social media and official channels, and investigated again after arrival, including places near a planned must-see. Social media could approve or reject a final choice. They name four roles: need generator, supporter, guider, and approver. The authors say most respondents are of Asian origin and that the Western subsample is too small for a cultural comparison. This is not a study of walking or of one city.

**S016.** Huertas and Orden-Mejía (2022) surveyed 402 people online about digital searches during their last trip. The sample was gathered through university contacts. Of that sample, 371 people, 92.2%, said they searched for tourist attractions while at the destination, a higher share than searched for transport or restaurants. For attractions, the leading channel in their results was Google, then tourism websites and Google Maps. Social networks and apps were used less often for that topic. Transport searches were led by Google Maps, and restaurant searches by TripAdvisor. The authors treat this as recall of a past trip, not as observation of an app session. Heritage cities were the most common destination type in the sample, at 38.6%, so the result is not only about cities and not only about walking.

Attraction discovery inside Smart Travel Companion is still a **HYPOTHESIS**. These two studies describe information search. They do not test this product.

### RQ2 — phone use and walking, added check

**S017.** Zhang et al. (2023) meta-analysed 22 studies with 592 participants. Using a phone while walking was associated with slower gait and shorter steps. Texting and reading changed gait velocity more than calling, talking, or dialling. Step length and step time were not affected in the same way for older adults as for younger adults. The authors say the task and the setting matter. This is pedestrian safety research, not tourism research. It does not by itself justify a glasses feature.

### Product documentation, not usability tests

**S009 and S010, Google.** Official help states that Google Maps directions can be started for a walking figure as well as other modes, that navigation needs GPS, location, and audio permission, and that voice guidance can be muted or limited to alerts. On Android, “Detailed voice guidance” under walking options speaks the distance to the next turn. TalkBack is supported. Lens in Maps with TalkBack is not supported on tablet-size devices. Wheelchair-accessible transit can be filtered. Wheelchair-accessible place attributes, where offered, cover entrance, restroom, seating, parking, and elevator, and the help page says this place information is available only in certain countries. Text size is changed through the phone’s accessibility settings. The navigation page says battery use rises because the app uses location, the screen, and the speakers, and that the screen can be turned off while voice continues. The page mentions adding more destinations only as a link. That linked article was not opened, so a multi-stop sightseeing flow is **not verified**.

**S011–S013, Wanderlog.** Help articles state that an itinerary can show time and distance between places by driving, walking, or public transit. Pro members can optimise one day’s order to reduce travel time and fuel use, and can keep a downloaded trip, including notes, places, and attachments, without a connection. These pages do not document turn-by-turn street guidance or a smart-glasses mode.

## 7. Contradictory findings and research limitations

- S004 finds a memory advantage for AR glasses over phones on one short residential path. S005 finds that outdoor AR performance advantages are less consistent than indoor ones. Both can be true: a memory benefit is not the same as a faster or more reliable outdoor route.
- S005 reports that reviewed studies often find lower cognitive load for AR than for other guidance, while also warning that extra information and unfamiliar hardware can raise load. S006 says making navigation easier can also reduce attention to the environment. “Less load” is not automatically a better tourist experience.
- S004’s authors link phone use to looking down. That is their interpretation of their result, tested on a landmark-based prototype, not on Google Maps or Wanderlog.
- S014’s GPS phone is a 2008 research device with a small map, not a current tourist app.
- S007’s 35 studies often give little information about participants’ visual acuity, which the authors say limits transfer to a varied population. Assistive apps in that review supplement cane or dog travel. They do not replace it.
- None of the opened experiments is a study of holiday sightseeing, ticket purchase, or a premium glasses subscription.
- Interviews were not conducted. Competitor apps were not used in a logged session.

## 9. Accessibility considerations

The brief still requires one persona with an impairment or disability and mobile accessibility considerations. Choose the impairment from sources you can explain. Do not invent a life story.

**S007** is the strongest opened source for that persona. It reviews 35 peer-reviewed studies from 2016 to February 2021 of wayfinding devices used by people who are blind, have low vision, or are deafblind in real indoor and outdoor settings. Thirty-three of the 35 used a smart device. The authors’ synthesis says travellers needed:

- control of the speed and amount of guidance
- custom audio, visual, and haptic output
- a preview before travel and live information during travel
- devices that can sit on the body without losing access when the person moves

The same paper says real-time route information was associated with higher success in the studies reviewed, and that some participants found apps reduced what they had to remember while others reported more cognitive load. It also warns against using blindfolded sighted people as a substitute for people with visual impairments.

**S006** adds that sensory, cognitive, and physical abilities need separate attention, and that physical and cognitive accessibility have less navigation research than low vision. Wheelchair-friendly routing is one of the nine principles. That is a reason to consider motor impairment only if you find and read more than this single mention.

**S008** WCAG 2.2, W3C Recommendation dated 12 December 2024 on the page opened, applies to web content on phones as well as larger screens. It aims to cover blindness, low vision, deafness, hearing loss, limited movement, speech disabilities, photosensitivity, and some cognitive and learning needs, and it states that it does not cover every need. It is a design standard, not evidence of a tourist’s experience. Use it when you judge an interface. Do not cite it as a user study.

**S009** shows one commercial response to wheelchair access and screen-reader use. Availability is limited by country and by device size for Lens. That is a product constraint to check in your own session.

## 10. Possible design opportunities

**HYPOTHESIS, for you to test against the evidence:**

- A walking view that leads with one landmark-based instruction and keeps a separate overview, because S006 separates turn information from global orientation and warns against clutter.
- Spoken guidance with the screen available rather than required, because S009 already offers walking voice guidance and S004 associates looking at a phone with weaker landmark memory.
- A glasses layer with very little text, placed out of the centre of view, because S004’s prototype used bottom-centre placement and S005 reports interface and outdoor-performance problems. This does not validate a paid glasses feature.
- Access information on the place and on the walking link, because S006 and S009 treat physical access as distinct from general place ratings.
- A plan that can be reviewed before walking and followed in short updates, because S007 distinguishes preview from live guidance.

None of these is a Must requirement until you write it in the MoSCoW table with a source and a rationale.

## 11. Unvalidated assumptions

- That city tourists discover landmarks poorly. S015 and S016 show that people search before and during a trip, and that channels differ by topic. They do not show that current tools fail.
- That tourists want one app rather than a map plus a guide. S007’s multiple-app point is about blind and deafblind participants in cited focus groups.
- That smart glasses are the right premium feature for this audience. S004 and S005 support further design attention. They do not show demand, price, or safety in crowds.
- That a 2008 GPS result still describes Google Maps or Wanderlog.
- The specific impairment, age, trip length, and language of the three personas.
- Any emotional journey, quotation, or star rating.
